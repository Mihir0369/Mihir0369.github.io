Banking · Retail lending

The strongest negative association between rate changes and credit growth appears at an 18-month lag: what does that mean for lenders?

When the Bank of Canada raises interest rates, borrowing is supposed to cool. I used public Bank of Canada and Statistics Canada data from 2010 to 2026 to look at the lag at which credit growth is most associated with earlier rate changes (tested from 0 to 24 months), and whether families ended up spending more of their income on debt payments. The data is observational, so it shows association, not cause.

Bottom line: the strongest negative association between rate changes and credit growth appears at an 18-month lag, across lags of 0 to 24 months. It is observational: it depends on the method and window, and the data cannot show cause, so lenders should test it before planning around it.

Executive summary

Business problemAt what lag are household borrowing and debt-service burden most associated with earlier Bank of Canada rate changes, and in which credit categories?
Dataset and scopeBank of Canada Valet API and Statistics Canada tables 36-10-0639, 38-10-0238 and 11-10-0065 (real, market-level). 202 months of rates, 8 credit categories and 66 quarters of debt service.
My contributionEnd to end: pulled the data from two public sources, checked it in Python, built the star-schema model and DAX, designed the five-page report and wrote the findings
Key finding18 months is the lag with the strongest negative association between rate changes and credit growth (correlation -0.52, tested at lags of 0 to 24 months; observational, not causal)
Recommended business actionTest the 18-month lag before planning around it. The strongest association appears at 18 months, but it depends on the method and window and is not proof of cause. Compare lending targets with rate moves 12 to 24 months earlier as a hypothesis to test.
LimitationsCorrelation is not causation: many things move at once when rates change. Rates and credit both trend and the 12-month windows overlap, so the lag is a pattern, not a precise delay.

Key findings

  1. Household credit grew from $1.48 trillion in January 2010 to $3.30 trillion in August 2026, 2.22 times as large.
  2. Credit growth peaked at 8.69% in May 2022 and slowed to 3.48% by October 2023, in the period when the prime rate rose from 2.45% to 7.20%. Other factors also changed.
  3. Across lags of 0 to 24 months, the strongest negative correlation between rate changes and credit growth is -0.52, at 18 months. At 6 months it is only -0.17. It is an observational association, not proof of cause.
  4. Debt payments took 15.17% of household income in early 2023, up from 12.56% in the April 2020 quarter, and were 14.52% in the latest quarter (2026 Q2, starting April 2026).

Data coverage

Source organizationBank of Canada (Valet API) and Statistics Canada tables 36-10-0639, 38-10-0238 and 11-10-0065
Covered period and latest observationsJanuary 2010 onward. Latest observation by source: household credit August 2026, interest rates October 2026, debt service the quarter starting April 2026 (2026 Q2). The sources are not current to the same date
GrainMonthly (rates, credit by category) and quarterly (debt service); weekly rates are rolled up to the last weekly value of each month
DenominatorCredit growth is year over year on household credit balances. Debt service is a share of household income
Exclusions and gapsAuto loans start in 2016. Credit-card and personal-loan growth spikes in 2011 to 2014 look like reclassifications
Known limitationsMarket-level data with no customer records. Sources differ in frequency and release schedule, and the lag result is observational

Limits of this analysis

  • Correlation is not causation: many things move at once when rates change.
  • Rates and credit both trend and the 12-month windows overlap, so the lag is a pattern, not a precise delay.
  • The data is national and aggregated, so it says nothing about individual borrowers or default risk.
  • I did not find a Statistics Canada table with delinquency or arrears, so the project does not report it.
  • Credit data ends in August 2026, rates in October 2026 and debt service in April 2026.
  • Credit cards and personal loans show jumps in 2011 to 2014 that look like reclassifications, so they are used as latest-month figures only.
